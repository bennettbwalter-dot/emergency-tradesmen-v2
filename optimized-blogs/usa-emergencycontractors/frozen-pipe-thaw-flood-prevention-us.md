    <h2>2. The Expansion Trap: Why Pipes Burst During the Melt</h2>
    <div class="capsule-box">
        <ul>
            <li><strong>Hydraulic Pressure:</strong> Ice forms a plug. When more ice grows, it squeezes the liquid water against the closed faucet until the pipe wall fails.</li>
            <li><strong>The "Invisible" Fracture:</strong> The pipe has already burst during the night; you just don't know it yet because the ice is acting as a "cork."</li>
            <li><strong>Flash Thaw:</strong> A rapid 20-degree jump in 4 hours is the highest risk period for US plumbing systems.</li>
        </ul>
    </div>
    <p>Most American homeowners are shocked when water starts spraying during a warm afternoon. They think "But the temperature is 40 degrees!" That warming is exactly the problem. As the ice plug melts, the water behind it-which has been under thousands of pounds of pressure-finally finds its way out of the crack created during the deep freeze. This is why you must treat every frozen pipe as a "potential burst" until it has been fully thawed and inspected by a licensed pro.</p>

    <h2>3. Emergency Response: Main Shut-off Valve Locations</h2>
    <div class="capsule-box">
        <ul>
            <li><strong>Basement:</strong> Look for the valve on the street-facing wall, usually right before the water meter.</li>
            <li><strong>Slab Homes:</strong> Often located in the garage or a utility closet near the water heater.</li>
            <li><strong>Curbside:</strong> If you can't find an internal valve, find the meter pit in your front yard near the sidewalk. You may need a "Meter Key" to turn it.</li>
        </ul>
    </div>
    <p>In a US plumbing emergency, time is your enemy. A 3/4-inch pipe can dump 250 gallons in a single day-enough to fill your basement to your knees. In 2026, ensure your valve is clearly labeled with a red tag. If you have an outdoor meter pit, check it today for bird nests or debris that might slow you down. If your house has a "Gate Valve" (the round handle), consider having it replaced with a "Ball Valve" (the lever handle), which is much less likely to jam after years of non-use.</p>

    <h2>6. Gentle Thawing: Hairdryers vs. Propane Torches</h2>
    <div class="capsule-box">
        <ul>
            <li><strong>Safe:</strong> Hairdryers, space heaters, or hot-water-soaked towels.</li>
            <li><strong>Dangerous:</strong> Propane torches, kerosene heaters, or any open flame.</li>
            <li><strong>Method:</strong> Start thawing at the faucet end of the pipe and work your way toward the freeze. Keep the faucet OPEN.</li>
        </ul>
    </div>
    <p>Never, ever use a torch to thaw a pipe. The rapid temperature change can cause "Thermal Shock," making the metal brittle and more likely to shatter. Even worse, the flame can ignite the insulation or the dry wood studs inside your US home's walls. In 2026, the safest way is to use a <strong>UL-Listed "Heat Tape"</strong> or a hairdryer on low heat. If you can't reach the pipe, leave the heater on in that room and open the cabinet doors to let the house warmth reach the plumbing.</p>

    <h2>7. IPC & UPC 2026: The New Rules for Exterior Walls</h2>

            <li><strong>Heat Trace:</strong> If a pipe must be exposed, it must use <strong>NEC-compliant</strong> heat trace with Ground Fault protection (GFEP).</li>
        </ul>
    </div>

If you are renovating your kitchen or bathroom, ensure your plumber follows the "Frost Line" rules for your specific US state. Any pipe in an exterior wall must be insulated with closed-cell foam to prevent the "Melt-and-Burst" cycle that follows the Northern winter.</p>

    <h2>8. Crawlspace Risks: Insulation and Draft Sealing</h2>

            <li><strong>Lagging:</strong> All crawlspace pipes must be wrapped in R-4 or higher insulation.</li>
            <li><strong>Draft Sealing:</strong> Use spray foam to seal the "Rim Joist" where the house meets the foundation.</li>
