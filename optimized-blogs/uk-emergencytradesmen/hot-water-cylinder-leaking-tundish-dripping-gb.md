<main class="blog-magazine-wrap">
<p class="blog-intro">You open the airing cupboard to grab a towel and hear a sharp tick, tick, tick. The shelf feels warm. The floorboard under the cylinder has a dark wet patch. Then you spot it: water running through the little plastic tundish pipe like a tiny tap you never turned on.</p>

    <p>That moment can make a calm person start moving towels around like sandbags. A hot water cylinder holds a lot of stored heat and pressure. If it leaks from the wrong place, you do not poke at valves and hope for the best.</p>

    <p>Slow down. You can still protect the cupboard, stop fresh water feeding the problem, and decide whether the fault needs a washer, a valve, or a proper cylinder replacement. The trick is knowing which checks are safe for you and which ones belong to a qualified plumber.</p>

    <h2>1. Hot Water Cylinder Leaks: The Knowledge Capsule Summary</h2>
    <div class="capsule-box">
        <ul>
            <li><strong>Do Not Cap a Tundish:</strong> A dripping tundish is a safety discharge path, not a nuisance overflow to block or tape shut.</li>
            <li><strong>Turn Down Risk:</strong> Switch off the immersion heater or programmer if water is near wiring, switches, or the cylinder stat.</li>
            <li><strong>Check the Source:</strong> Leaks can come from compression joints, expansion valves, immersion seals, cylinder seams, or the discharge pipework.</li>
            <li><strong>Pressure Matters:</strong> Repeated tundish discharge often points to expansion vessel, PRV, or temperature and pressure relief valve faults.</li>
            <li><strong>Save Your Wallet:</strong> A valve or washer may be repairable. A split cylinder or failed unvented safety control needs replacement before water damages the airing cupboard and ceiling.</li>
        </ul>
    </div>

    <h2>2. Step 1: Make the Area Safe and Stop Fresh Water</h2>
    <div class="blog-step-card">
        <div class="blog-step-header">
            <span class="blog-step-number">Step 1</span>
            <h2>Make the Cupboard Safe</h2>
        </div>
        <div class="blog-step-body">
            <p>Keep children and pets away from the airing cupboard. Hot water from a cylinder or tundish can scald. Do not touch copper pipes, the immersion heater boss, or any valve body until you know it has cooled.</p>
            <p>If water is spreading across the floor, turn off the cold feed to the cylinder. Many homes have a small isolation valve on the cold pipe entering the cylinder. If you cannot find it, turn off the main stopcock under the kitchen sink or near the front door. Turn it clockwise until it stops. Then open a hot tap downstairs to reduce pressure in the hot pipework.</p>
            <p>If water is near electrics, switch off the immersion heater at its fused spur. If you see water close to the consumer unit, sockets, or visible wiring, stop and call an emergency electrician as well as a plumber. Water and electricity do not negotiate.</p>
            <p>Put a bowl under a drip if you can reach it without touching hot metal. Take photos before you mop. Photos help a plumber see where the leak started after the area dries.</p>
        </div>
    </div>

    <h2>3. Step 2: Find the Leak Source Without Taking Valves Apart</h2>
    <div class="blog-step-card">
        <div class="blog-step-header">
            <span class="blog-step-number">Step 2</span>
            <h2>Find the Leak Source</h2>
        </div>
        <div class="blog-step-body">
            <p>Look, do not dismantle. A cylinder leak can come from several places, and each one tells a different story.</p>
            <p>A drip from the pipework joint may mean a compression nut has loosened with expansion and contraction. A leak from the immersion heater boss may mean the seal has failed. A wet patch from the cylinder seam or base points to internal corrosion. A steady stream through the tundish often means a pressure relief valve, expansion vessel, or temperature control fault has opened the safety discharge.</p>
            <p>The tundish is the visible air gap on an unvented cylinder discharge pipe. During normal use, you should see no water running through it. A single short discharge after heavy hot water use can happen. Regular dripping needs attention.</p>
            <p>Do not cap, block, tape, or divert the discharge pipe. That pipe exists to move hot water and pressure away from the cylinder. Blocking it can turn a repair bill into a serious safety risk.</p>
        </div>
    </div>

    <h2>4. Step 3: Contain the Water and Reduce Heat Demand</h2>
    <div class="blog-step-card">
        <div class="blog-step-header">
            <span class="blog-step-number">Step 3</span>
            <h2>Contain Water and Heat</h2>
        </div>
        <div class="blog-step-body">
            <p>Once you have isolated the cold feed, turn off timed hot water demand at the programmer. If the cylinder uses an immersion heater, leave it off. If a boiler heats the cylinder, set hot water to off on the programmer and avoid running hot taps except to relieve pressure.</p>
            <p>Move towels, cardboard boxes, spare bedding, and cleaning products away from the airing cupboard floor. Use a shallow tray or bowl under a visible drip, but do not build a tower of towels around hot pipework. Wet towels trap heat and hide the leak path.</p>
            <p>If the cylinder sits above a ceiling, check the room below. Look for staining, bubbling paint, damp plaster, or water around light fittings. Do not switch lights on if water has reached them. Use a torch from outside the room and call an electrician.</p>
            <p>You can also mark the water level in a bowl with tape and note the time. A slow drip that fills a mug overnight is a different callout from a tundish flow that fills a bucket in ten minutes.</p>
        </div>
    </div>

    <h2>5. Step 4: Check the Safe, Low-Cost Faults First</h2>
    <div class="blog-step-card">
        <div class="blog-step-header">
            <span class="blog-step-number">Step 4</span>
            <h2>Check Low-Cost Faults</h2>
        </div>
        <div class="blog-step-body">
            <p>Some cylinder leaks are small plumbing jobs. A weep from a compression joint may need a new olive or a refitted joint. A leaking automatic air vent may need replacement. A loose drain cock may need a washer. Those fixes can cost far less than a new cylinder.</p>
            <p>With unvented cylinders, the safety gear matters. A dripping tundish can point to a failed expansion vessel, a faulty pressure reducing valve, or a pressure relief valve that has grit on its seat. A plumber may recharge the expansion vessel, clean or replace a valve, and test the discharge pipe. That is repair territory if the cylinder body remains sound.</p>
            <p>Do not try to reset or strip safety valves yourself. Do not twist the red discharge valve to &quot;see what happens&quot;. If the valve fails to reseat, you can create a faster leak. If someone installed the cylinder without correct discharge routing, you need a qualified tradesman to put it right.</p>
            <p>Save Your Wallet rule: pay for diagnosis before replacement. A cylinder that drips from a valve may need a valve. A cylinder that leaks from the shell needs replacement.</p>
        </div>
    </div>

    <h2>6. Step 5: Call the Right Tradesman Before the Damage Spreads</h2>
    <div class="blog-step-card">
        <div class="blog-step-header">
            <span class="blog-step-number">Step 5</span>
            <h2>Book the Right Tradesman</h2>
        </div>
        <div class="blog-step-body">
            <p>Call an emergency plumber if water keeps running through the tundish, the cylinder casing leaks, the stopcock is seized, the airing cupboard floor is soaked, or water has reached a ceiling below. For an unvented cylinder, ask for a plumber qualified to work on unvented hot water systems. Gas Safe matters if the same visit involves the boiler or gas appliance, but the cylinder work itself needs the right hot water qualification.</p>
            <p>Tell the plumber what you have done: cold feed isolated, immersion off, hot water programme off, bowl fill rate, and whether the discharge is hot or cold. Mention the cylinder brand and age if the label is visible.</p>
            <p>Do not agree to a full cylinder swap over the phone unless the casing has split, the base is leaking, or the unit shows heavy corrosion. A good tradesman will inspect the pipework, test pressure, and explain the repair vs. replace choice before quoting.</p>
        </div>
    </div>

    <h2>7. Practical Advice to Prevent Future Cylinder Leaks</h2>
    <div class="blog-tips-block">
            <p>Exercise the main stopcock twice a year so it does not seize when you need it. Turn it closed and open again, then leave it backed off a quarter turn. Label the cold feed isolation valve in the airing cupboard if you have one.</p>
            <p>Keep the cylinder area clear. You need space to see valves, the tundish, and the floor. Piling towels and boxes around the cylinder traps damp and hides warning signs.</p>
            <p>Book an annual check for unvented cylinders. Ask the plumber to test the expansion vessel, pressure reducing valve, discharge pipe, and thermostat cut-out. If your home has hard water, ask about scale protection on the cold feed. Scale and grit shorten valve life.</p>
            <p>Insulate nearby pipework with lagging, but keep safety valves and the tundish visible. A neat airing cupboard beats a warm cupboard that hides a leak until the ceiling stains.</p>
    </div>

    <h2>8. When to Call a Professional (And When to Repair vs. Replace)</h2>
    <div class="blog-when-block">
        <h2>Cylinder Problems That Need a Plumber</h2>
        <ul>
            <li><strong>Running Tundish:</strong> Water passes through the tundish for more than a few minutes or returns after cooling.</li>
            <li><strong>Wet Immersion Area:</strong> Water reaches the immersion heater cover, thermostat, flex, or nearby switch.</li>
            <li><strong>Cylinder Body Leak:</strong> Water comes from the metal casing, base, seam, or swollen insulation jacket.</li>
            <li><strong>Unsafe Discharge:</strong> The discharge pipe terminates in the wrong place or cannot be traced safely.</li>
        </ul>
    </div>

    <div class="blog-comparison-grid">
        <div class="blog-comp-col green">
            <div class="blog-comp-header">Repair the Controls</div>
            <ul class="blog-comp-list">
                <li>Replacing a failed valve, washer, air vent, or PRV</li>
                <li>Recharging or replacing an expansion vessel</li>
                <li>Repairing a leaking compression joint</li>
                <li>Correcting visible pipework around the tundish</li>
            </ul>
        </div>
        <div class="blog-comp-col red">
            <div class="blog-comp-header">Replace the Cylinder</div>
            <ul class="blog-comp-list">
                <li>Leak from the cylinder body or base</li>
                <li>Corroded casing or swollen insulation jacket</li>
                <li>Missing or unsafe unvented safety controls</li>
                <li>Repair cost close to a modern replacement</li>
            </ul>
        </div>
    </div>
    <p><strong>Save Your Wallet: The Quick Rule.</strong> Repair valves, joints, and controls. Replace a leaking cylinder body. If water comes through the tundish, diagnose the safety set first. If water comes through the metal casing, stop spending on patch jobs.</p>

    <h2>9. Quick Q&amp;A: Real Questions Homeowners Ask</h2>
    <div class="blog-faq-block">
            <h3>Q: Is a dripping tundish an emergency?</h3>
            <p>Yes, if it keeps dripping or the flow increases. The tundish shows that a safety discharge path has opened. Turn off heat demand, reduce pressure by opening a hot tap, and book an emergency callout.</p>
            <h3>Q: Can I keep using hot water if the cylinder is leaking?</h3>
            <p>No. Turn off the hot water programme or immersion heater until a plumber checks it. Heat can raise pressure and make the discharge worse.</p>
            <h3>Q: Why is water leaking from the airing cupboard ceiling below?</h3>
            <p>The cylinder, valves, or pipe joints may have leaked long enough to soak the floor. Turn off the stopcock, avoid lights in the wet room below, and call a plumber. Call an electrician if water has reached fittings.</p>
            <h3>Q: How long should a hot water cylinder last?</h3>
            <p>Many cylinders last 10 to 20 years with clean water, correct pressure, and regular checks. Hard water, poor installation, and failed expansion controls shorten that life.</p>
    </div>

    <h2>10. Two Quick Fun Facts</h2>
    <div class="fun-fact">
            <ul>
                <li>A typical UK hot water cylinder can store 120 to 210 litres. That is enough water to damage ceilings, flooring, and electrics if a leak runs through the night.</li>
                <li>The tundish gives you a visible warning before hidden discharge pipework carries water away. Treat it like an alarm, not a drain.</li>
            </ul>
