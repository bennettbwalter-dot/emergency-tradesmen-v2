<main class="blog-magazine-wrap">
<p class="blog-intro">You open the airing cupboard for a towel and hear a low hum from the heating pump. Then you spot water on the shelf, a green stain on a valve, and a drip heading towards the floorboards.</p>

    <p>A central heating pump can leak from tired pump valves, a split washer, a corroded body, trapped air, a seized motor, or pipework that has started to move under heat. The leak may look small, but water and electrics share that cupboard far too often.</p>

    <p>Do not poke the pump, remove covers, or keep turning the heating on to see if the noise clears. You can make the area safer, protect the boiler, and decide whether a same-day repair will stop the leak or the pump needs replacement.</p>

    <h2>1. Heating Pump Leak: The Knowledge Capsule Summary</h2>
    <div class="capsule-box">
        <ul>
            <li><strong>Switch the Heating Off:</strong> Turn the programmer or room thermostat down so the pump stops calling for heat.</li>
            <li><strong>Keep Electrics Dry:</strong> If water sits near a fused spur, cable, or socket, leave it alone and book an emergency callout.</li>
            <li><strong>Catch the Drip:</strong> Use a bowl and towels if you can reach the leak without touching hot pipework.</li>
            <li><strong>Do Not Force Valves:</strong> Old pump valves can shear, leak faster, or seize half shut.</li>
            <li><strong>Save Your Wallet:</strong> A washer or valve may fix a small leak. A seized, noisy, or corroded pump often needs replacement.</li>
        </ul>
    </div>

    <h2>2. Step 1: Turn the Heating Demand Off</h2>
    <div class="blog-step-card">
        <div class="blog-step-header">
            <span class="blog-step-number">Step 1</span>
            <h2>Stop the Pump Before the Leak Spreads</h2>
        </div>
        <div class="blog-step-body">
            <p>Turn the heating and hot water controls off at the programmer. If your system has a room thermostat, turn it down as well.</p>
            <p>Let the pipework cool before you put towels near the pump. Heating pipes can burn skin, and hot water can run down the outside of a valve.</p>
            <p>If the pump keeps humming after the controls are off, do not hit it or twist the screw in the middle. A seized motor or stuck relay needs testing.</p>
            <p>If water drips onto electrical fittings, step back. A plumber or heating engineer can isolate the system without putting you near live parts.</p>
        </div>
    </div>

    <h2>3. Step 2: Find the Drip Without Taking the Pump Apart</h2>
    <div class="blog-step-card">
        <div class="blog-step-header">
            <span class="blog-step-number">Step 2</span>
            <h2>Use Sight, Sound, and Photos</h2>
        </div>
        <div class="blog-step-body">
            <p>Look for water at the two pump valves, the pump body, the bleed screw, and the pipe joints above and below the pump.</p>
            <p>Green, white, or rusty staining can show an older slow leak. Fresh shiny water usually points to the part that failed today.</p>
            <p>Take photos from a safe distance. Include the pump label, valve position, boiler make if you can see it, and any water marks inside the airing cupboard.</p>
            <p>Do not remove the pump head or open the bleed screw. Water can run fast once pressure finds a gap.</p>
        </div>
    </div>

    <h2>4. Step 3: Protect the Floor and Nearby Storage</h2>
    <div class="blog-step-card">
        <div class="blog-step-header">
            <span class="blog-step-number">Step 3</span>
            <h2>Keep Water Away From Timber and Wiring</h2>
        </div>
        <div class="blog-step-body">
            <p>Move towels, bedding, paperwork, and cleaning products out of the airing cupboard. Wet fabric hides the size of the leak and can keep timber wet for days.</p>
            <p>Put a shallow tray or bowl under the drip if the area is cool and safe to reach. Change towels before they soak through to the floorboards.</p>
            <p>Check the room below for damp patches, stains, or a bulge in the ceiling. A slow heating leak can travel along pipes before it shows.</p>
            <p>If the leak starts to run rather than drip, find the stopcock if you know it affects your system, then call for help. Some heating systems still hold water after the mains supply has been shut.</p>
        </div>
    </div>

    <h2>5. Step 4: Check the Boiler and Pressure Gauge</h2>
    <div class="blog-step-card">
        <div class="blog-step-header">
            <span class="blog-step-number">Step 4</span>
            <h2>Do Not Keep Repressurising the System</h2>
        </div>
        <div class="blog-step-body">
            <p>If you have a combi boiler, check the pressure gauge from the front panel. Low pressure after a pump leak means the system has lost water.</p>
            <p>Do not keep topping up the boiler to restore heating. Extra water can feed the leak and may hide the fault from the engineer.</p>
            <p>If the boiler shows a fault code, write it down before switching anything off. A Gas Safe engineer may need that code if the fault affects the boiler case, flue, or gas appliance.</p>
            <p>If the boiler smells burnt, bangs, or leaks from inside its casing, leave the casing shut and book an emergency callout.</p>
        </div>
    </div>

    <h2>6. Step 5: Book the Right Emergency Callout</h2>
    <div class="blog-step-card">
        <div class="blog-step-header">
            <span class="blog-step-number">Step 5</span>
            <h2>Give the Heating Details That Save Time</h2>
        </div>
        <div class="blog-step-body">
            <p>Book an emergency callout if the pump leaks, hums without moving water, trips electrics, leaves you without heating or hot water, or sends water towards a ceiling.</p>
            <p>Tell the dispatcher whether the pump sits in an airing cupboard, loft, utility space, or beside the boiler. Mention whether you have a cylinder, combi boiler, or sealed heating system.</p>
            <p>Ask whether the tradesman can isolate pump valves, drain enough water to work cleanly, replace washers, fit a new pump, test circulation, and check for air locks.</p>
            <p>If the work touches the boiler casing or gas controls, ask for a Gas Safe engineer. If it affects fixed electrics, ask whether a NICEIC or NAPIT electrician needs to attend.</p>
        </div>
    </div>

    <h2>7. Practical Advice to Prevent Future Heating Pump Faults</h2>
    <div class="blog-tips-block">
            <p>Listen for a new hum, buzz, or grinding sound when the heating starts. A quiet pump that becomes noisy may have air, sludge, or worn bearings.</p>
            <p>Check the airing cupboard once a month during heating season. Look for green marks, damp timber, or crust around pump valves.</p>
            <p>Ask for inhibitor checks when the system gets serviced. Dirty water and sludge can wear pumps and block radiators.</p>
            <p>Do not stack towels tight around the pump. Heat needs space to leave the cupboard, and fabric can hide leaks.</p>
    </div>

    <h2>8. When to Call a Professional (And When to Repair vs. Replace)</h2>
    <div class="blog-when-block">
        <h2>Heating Pump Faults That Need a Tradesman</h2>
        <ul>
            <li><strong>Water Near Electrics:</strong> Any drip close to a fused spur, cable, or socket needs urgent help.</li>
            <li><strong>No Heat or Hot Water:</strong> A humming pump may have seized or failed to circulate water.</li>
            <li><strong>Boiler Pressure Drops:</strong> Falling pressure after a pump leak means the system is losing water.</li>
            <li><strong>Corroded Valves:</strong> Old pump valves can break when someone tries to isolate them.</li>
        </ul>
    </div>

    <div class="blog-comparison-grid">
        <div class="blog-comp-col green">
            <div class="blog-comp-header">Repair the Fault</div>
            <ul class="blog-comp-list">
                <li>Loose pump union on sound pipework</li>
                <li>Split washer with clean pump valves</li>
                <li>Air in the pump after recent heating work</li>
                <li>Minor seep from a valve that can be rebuilt</li>
            </ul>
        </div>
        <div class="blog-comp-col red">
            <div class="blog-comp-header">Replace the Part</div>
            <ul class="blog-comp-list">
                <li>Corroded pump body</li>
                <li>Seized motor that hums but does not circulate</li>
                <li>Leaking isolation valves that will not shut</li>
                <li>Heat-damaged wiring or a failed fused spur</li>
            </ul>
        </div>
    </div>
    <p><strong>Save Your Wallet: The Quick Rule.</strong> Repair a washer, union, or trapped-air issue when the pump tests sound. Replace a seized, corroded, or leaking pump before it ruins the airing cupboard, boiler pressure, or ceiling below.</p>

    <h2>9. Quick Q&amp;A: Real Questions Homeowners Ask</h2>
    <div class="blog-faq-block">
            <h3>Q: Can I still use the heating if the pump only drips?</h3>
            <p>No. Turn the heating demand off and get the leak checked before water reaches electrics or lowers boiler pressure.</p>
            <h3>Q: Can I tighten the big nuts on either side of the pump?</h3>
            <p>Do not force them. Old valves and unions can crack, and a small drip can become a fast leak.</p>
            <h3>Q: Does a humming pump mean the boiler has failed?</h3>
            <p>The pump, valves, air, sludge, wiring, or controls may have caused the noise. An engineer can test circulation before anyone blames the boiler.</p>
            <h3>Q: Should I call a plumber or Gas Safe engineer?</h3>
            <p>Call an emergency plumber or heating engineer for the pump. Ask for Gas Safe cover if the work involves the boiler casing or gas controls.</p>
    </div>

    <h2>10. Two Quick Fun Facts</h2>
    <div class="fun-fact">
            <ul>
                <li>A small heating pump moves hot water through many radiators, so one seized motor can make a whole home feel cold.</li>
                <li>Green staining near copper joints often comes from slow water contact with copper salts.</li>
            </ul>
