<main class="blog-magazine-wrap">
    <p class="blog-intro">You turn the key in the front door and feel the snap before you hear it. Half the key sits in your hand. The rest is buried in the lock, and the shopping is still on the path.</p>

    <p>A broken key can trap you outside, lock someone inside, or leave the door stuck half-secure. The damage may be a tired key, a worn euro cylinder, a misaligned door, or a gearbox that has started to bind.</p>

    <p>Do not poke the keyway with a screwdriver or force the handle until it gives. You can keep the door safe, check whether another entrance is secure, gather useful details, and decide whether an emergency locksmith can extract the key or should replace the cylinder.</p>

    <h2>1. Snapped Key Emergency: The Knowledge Capsule Summary</h2>
    <div class="capsule-box">
        <ul>
            <li><strong>Stop Turning the Lock:</strong> Forcing the remaining key can push the broken piece deeper.</li>
            <li><strong>Keep the Door Secure:</strong> Stay near the door if it will not lock, or use another safe entrance if one exists.</li>
            <li><strong>Do Not Pick at It:</strong> Pins, knives, and drill bits can damage the lock and cost more than extraction.</li>
            <li><strong>Check Door Alignment:</strong> A door that needs lifting on the handle may be stressing the lock each day.</li>
            <li><strong>Save Your Wallet:</strong> A clean key extraction can be cheap. A worn cylinder or damaged gearbox needs replacement.</li>
        </ul>
    </div>

    <h2>2. Step 1: Stop Forcing the Key and Check Who Is at Risk</h2>
    <div class="blog-step-card">
        <div class="blog-step-header">
            <span class="blog-step-number">Step 1</span>
            <h2>Protect People Before Protecting the Lock</h2>
        </div>
        <div class="blog-step-body">
            <p>Step back from the door and check whether anyone is locked inside, including children, older relatives, or pets. If someone inside needs urgent help, call emergency services first.</p>
            <p>If the door is open but will not lock, do not leave it unattended. Ask a neighbour to stand with you if you need to move bags, tools, or work kit indoors.</p>
            <p>If the door is closed and you are locked out, check whether a back door, side gate, or neighbour-held spare key gives you safe access. Do not climb through upstairs windows or force a weak pane.</p>
            <p>Take a photo of the lock, handle, and broken key piece in your hand. The locksmith can often identify the cylinder style and bring the right parts.</p>
        </div>
    </div>

    <h2>3. Step 2: Do Not Push the Broken Piece Deeper</h2>
    <div class="blog-step-card">
        <div class="blog-step-header">
            <span class="blog-step-number">Step 2</span>
            <h2>Leave the Keyway Alone</h2>
        </div>
        <div class="blog-step-body">
            <p>A snapped key may sit close to the front of the cylinder, which gives a locksmith a fair chance of pulling it out with an extractor. Pushing wire, tweezers, or another key into the slot can bury it past the first pins.</p>
            <p>Do not spray oil into the lock. A small amount of graphite or lock lubricant can help in normal maintenance, but a wet, dirty keyway can make extraction harder on a callout.</p>
            <p>Do not drill the lock yourself. Anti-snap and anti-drill cylinders can resist poor drilling and damage the handle, door furniture, or multipoint strip.</p>
            <p>If the broken piece sticks out, do not grip it with pliers unless it slides out without force. Stop at the first sign of resistance.</p>
        </div>
    </div>

    <h2>4. Step 3: Work Out Why the Key Snapped</h2>
    <div class="blog-step-card">
        <div class="blog-step-header">
            <span class="blog-step-number">Step 3</span>
            <h2>Tell the Locksmith About Daily Clues</h2>
        </div>
        <div class="blog-step-body">
            <p>Think about the last few weeks. A key that needed wiggling, a handle that had to be lifted hard, or a door that caught at the top can all strain the lock.</p>
            <p>Check the broken key in your hand. A bent, cracked, thin, or badly copied key often snaps before the lock fails. Keep both pieces if you can.</p>
            <p>If the lock sits in a uPVC or composite door, note whether the multipoint hooks still move when the door is open. Do not keep lifting the handle if it feels stiff.</p>
            <p>Tell the locksmith whether the door has been slammed, adjusted, painted, or swollen in heat. A small alignment fault can turn a normal key turn into a broken key.</p>
        </div>
    </div>

    <h2>5. Step 4: Keep the Home Secure While You Wait</h2>
    <div class="blog-step-card">
        <div class="blog-step-header">
            <span class="blog-step-number">Step 4</span>
            <h2>Do Not Leave a Half-Locked Door</h2>
        </div>
        <div class="blog-step-body">
            <p>If you can get inside through another door, check whether the faulty front door can latch shut. Do not rely on a latch if the main lock will not throw.</p>
            <p>If the door cannot lock, keep someone at home until the locksmith arrives. Move valuables away from the hallway and keep the broken key out of sight.</p>
            <p>If the broken key leaves the door locked from the outside but openable from inside, do not post online that you are locked out. Share details only with the person coming to help.</p>
            <p>If your key snapped after an attempted break-in, take photos and call the police non-emergency line before repair if the scene is safe.</p>
        </div>
    </div>

    <h2>6. Step 5: Book an Emergency Callout With the Right Lock Details</h2>
    <div class="blog-step-card">
        <div class="blog-step-header">
            <span class="blog-step-number">Step 5</span>
            <h2>Ask for Extraction and Cylinder Options</h2>
        </div>
        <div class="blog-step-body">
            <p>Book an emergency callout if you are locked out, the door will not secure, someone is locked inside, or the broken key sits too deep to remove by hand.</p>
            <p>Ask for a locksmith who can attempt non-destructive entry, extract the key, inspect the cylinder, and check the handle, keeps, hinges, and multipoint strip.</p>
            <p>Tell the dispatcher the door type, lock brand if you can see it, whether the key turns at all, and whether you need a new set of keys today.</p>
            <p>If the lock needs replacement, ask about a British Standard or anti-snap euro cylinder that matches the door thickness and keeps the thumbturn or key-side you need.</p>
        </div>
    </div>

    <h2>7. Practical Advice to Prevent Future Snapped Keys</h2>
    <div class="blog-tips-block">
        <p>Replace bent, cracked, or cheap copied keys before they fail in the lock.</p>
        <p>Book same-day repair if you need to lift the handle hard or pull the door towards you before the key turns.</p>
        <p>Keep the door and frame aligned after hinge adjustment, new flooring, or a heatwave that makes the door swell.</p>
        <p>Use a locksmith-approved lubricant once or twice a year. Cooking oil and sprays that catch dust can make the cylinder worse.</p>
    </div>

    <h2>8. When to Call a Professional (And When to Repair vs. Replace)</h2>
    <div class="blog-when-block">
        <h2>Lock Faults That Need a Tradesman</h2>
        <ul>
            <li><strong>Locked Out:</strong> A snapped key with no spare access needs prompt non-destructive entry.</li>
            <li><strong>Door Will Not Secure:</strong> A front door that cannot lock leaves the home exposed.</li>
            <li><strong>Stiff Handle:</strong> A binding multipoint lock can snap another key after extraction.</li>
            <li><strong>Old Cylinder:</strong> Worn or poor-security cylinders may cost more to save than replace.</li>
        </ul>
    </div>

    <div class="blog-comparison-grid">
        <div class="blog-comp-col green">
            <div class="blog-comp-header">Repair the Fault</div>
            <ul class="blog-comp-list">
                <li>Broken key sits near the front of the keyway</li>
                <li>Cylinder turns cleanly after extraction</li>
                <li>Door alignment and handle movement test well</li>
                <li>You have a sound spare key cut from the original</li>
            </ul>
        </div>
        <div class="blog-comp-col red">
            <div class="blog-comp-header">Replace the Lock or Cylinder</div>
            <ul class="blog-comp-list">
                <li>Key snaps again or the cylinder keeps binding</li>
                <li>Lock shows attempted break-in damage</li>
                <li>Multipoint gearbox grinds or will not throw</li>
                <li>Old cylinder lacks anti-snap protection</li>
            </ul>
        </div>
    </div>
    <p><strong>Save Your Wallet: The Quick Rule.</strong> Extract the key when the cylinder and door move cleanly afterwards. Replace the cylinder or repair the door mechanism when stiffness, wear, or break-in damage caused the snap, or you may pay for a second callout after the next key fails.</p>

    <h2>9. Quick Q&amp;A: Real Questions Homeowners Ask</h2>
    <div class="blog-faq-block">
        <h3>Q: Can I pull a snapped key out with tweezers?</h3>
        <p>Only if the piece sticks out and slides free. If it resists, stop before you push it deeper or damage the pins.</p>
        <h3>Q: Will the locksmith have to drill the lock?</h3>
        <p>Not always. Many snapped keys can be extracted, but a damaged or high-security cylinder may need controlled replacement.</p>
        <h3>Q: Should I replace the lock after losing the other half of the key?</h3>
        <p>Replace or rekey it if you cannot account for the missing key or if the door security already feels weak.</p>
        <h3>Q: Does a snapped key mean the door is faulty?</h3>
        <p>Sometimes. A worn key can fail on its own, but a stiff handle, dropped door, or binding gearbox should be checked.</p>
    </div>

    <h2>10. Two Quick Fun Facts</h2>
    <div class="fun-fact">
        <ul>
            <li>A badly copied key can be a tiny fraction out and still turn the lock for months before it snaps.</li>
            <li>Many uPVC door lock problems start with hinge alignment, not the cylinder itself.</li>
        </ul>
