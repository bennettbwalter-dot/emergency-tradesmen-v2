<div class="blog-step-card">
  <div class="blog-step-header">
    <span class="blog-step-number">Step 1</span>
    <h2>Check Multiple Fixtures to Isolate the Issue</h2>
  </div>
  <div class="blog-step-body">
    <p>Low pressure in one faucet usually means a clogged aerator or a faulty valve. Low pressure everywhere points to a main supply issue. Turn on the kitchen sink, a bathroom faucet, the shower, and an outdoor spigot. If they’re all weak, the problem is upstream. If only one fixture is affected, clean the aerator or check the shut-off valve under that sink. This quick test saves you from chasing a house-wide emergency when it’s just a five-minute cleanout.</p>
  </div>
</div>

<div class="blog-step-card">
  <div class="blog-step-header">
    <span class="blog-step-number">Step 2</span>
    <h2>Inspect the Water Meter for Hidden Leaks</h2>
  </div>
  <div class="blog-step-body">
    <p>Your water meter is the truth-teller. Locate it (usually in a concrete box near the street or sidewalk). Make sure no water is running inside or outside the house. Watch the meter dial or digital readout. If it’s still moving, you have a leak. Many modern meters have a small triangle or gear that spins with even tiny flows. If it’s turning while everything is off, your main line or a hidden pipe is losing water. Note the reading, wait an hour without using water, and check again. A jumping number confirms a leak that needs attention.</p>
  </div>
</div>

<div class="blog-step-card">
  <div class="blog-step-header">
    <span class="blog-step-number">Step 3</span>
    <h2>Walk the Property for Wet Spots or Soggy Ground</h2>
  </div>
  <div class="blog-step-body">
    <p>Main line leaks often announce themselves through your landscaping. Walk your yard, driveway, and foundation line. Look for unexplained puddles, unusually green patches of grass, sinking soil, or water pooling near the street. In spring, ground shift and freezing/thawing cycles can crack older copper or PVC lines. If you see water bubbling up from the ground or hear a faint hissing near the meter box, do not dig. Call a licensed plumber with leak detection equipment. Excavating blindly can damage gas lines, electrical conduits, or fiber optics.</p>
  </div>
</div>

<div class="blog-step-card">
  <div class="blog-step-header">
    <span class="blog-step-number">Step 4</span>
    <h2>Check the Pressure Regulator & Main Shut-Off Valve</h2>
  </div>
  <div class="blog-step-body">
    <p>Many US homes have a pressure-reducing valve (PRV) near the main shut-off, usually where the water line enters the house or garage. If it fails, pressure can drop suddenly or spike dangerously. Check that the main shut-off valve is fully open. Sometimes it gets bumped during spring maintenance or irrigation startup. If you have a PRV and pressure is consistently below 40 PSI, the regulator may be failing. Do not attempt to adjust or replace it yourself. Incorrect settings can burst pipes or damage appliances.</p>
  </div>
</div>

<div class="blog-step-card">
  <div class="blog-step-header">
    <span class="blog-step-number">Step 5</span>
    <h2>Shut Off the Main & Call a Professional</h2>
  </div>
  <div class="blog-step-body">
    <p>If your meter is spinning, your yard is soggy, or pressure has dropped to a trickle, turn off the main water valve immediately. This stops further damage, prevents foundation erosion, and cuts your water bill from skyrocketing. Once the water is off, book a 24/7 emergency plumber. Main line repairs require specialized cameras, acoustic leak detectors, and trenchless or excavation equipment. A licensed contractor will locate the break, explain repair options, and restore your pressure safely.</p>
  </div>
</div>

<div class="blog-tips-block">
  <h2>Practical Tips to Prevent Main Line & Pressure Issues</h2>
  <ul>
    <li>Know where your main shut-off valve is and test it twice a year. It should turn smoothly without forcing.</li>
    <li>Install a whole-house pressure gauge. Healthy residential pressure sits between 50–70 PSI.</li>
    <li>Avoid planting trees near your water line. Roots seek moisture and can crack or crush pipes over time.</li>
    <li>Flush your system annually by opening the main drain valve (if equipped) to clear sediment that can clog regulators and valves.</li>
  </ul>
</div>

<div class="blog-qa-section">
  <h2>Quick Q&A: Real Questions Homeowners Ask</h2>
  <div class="blog-qa-card">
    <p class="blog-qa-q">Why did my water pressure drop all of a sudden?</p>
    <ul>
      <li>Failing pressure regulator or partially closed main valve</li>
      <li>Hidden leak in the main supply line or slab</li>
      <li>Municipal supply issue or neighborhood main break</li>
    </ul>

  <div class="blog-qa-card">
    <p class="blog-qa-q">Can a running toilet cause low water pressure?</p>
    <ul>
      <li>No. A running toilet wastes water but doesn’t drop whole-house pressure</li>
      <li>If pressure is low everywhere, the issue is upstream of fixtures</li>
      <li>Isolate the toilet valve to rule it out during testing</li>
    </ul>
  </div>
  <div class="blog-qa-card">
    <p class="blog-qa-q">How do plumbers find underground leaks?</p>
    <ul>
      <li>Acoustic listening devices detect hissing through soil</li>
      <li>Video camera inspection locates cracks or root intrusion</li>
      <li>Thermal imaging spots temperature differences from escaping water</li>
    </ul>
  </div>
