# Roadside Emergency: 7 Safety Protocols to Survive a Highway Breakdown

To survive a highway breakdown in the US, you must immediately pull your vehicle onto the right shoulder, as far from traffic as possible. Turn your wheels away from the road, activate your hazard lights, and wait inside the vehicle with your seatbelt fastened unless you can safely reach a grass verge or behind a guardrail. Call a licensed professional recovery technician immediately.

> **WARNING:** Roadside assistance is one of the most dangerous professions in America. One tow truck driver is killed every six days by motorists who fail to comply with "Slow Down, Move Over" laws. DIY repairs — like changing a tire on a high-speed interstate — are extremely high-risk and frequently fatal. Always call a licensed professional.

## Why This Is Dangerous

![A broken down vehicle on a high-speed motorway being passed by a large HGV](/blog/day38/uk-motorway-breakdown-hard-shoulder.jpg)

High-speed interstates are lethal environments for stationary vehicles. According to **AAA Foundation for Traffic Safety** data, nearly 90% of roadside fatalities occur in zones with speed limits of 55 mph or higher. A driver traveling at 70 mph covers over 100 feet per second — by the time they see your hazard lights, reaction time is nearly zero.

Secondary hazards include:
- **Move Over Law Violations:** Despite laws in all 50 states, many drivers do not shift lanes or reduce speed, causing over 60 tow operator deaths annually.
- **The "Moth" Effect:** Impaired or fatigued drivers tend to unconsciously steer toward lights on the shoulder — directly into your path.
- **Low-Visibility Strikes:** 63% of roadside collisions happen in darkness, where your silhouette becomes invisible against the glare of oncoming headlights.
- **Fast-Moving Debris:** At interstate speeds, even small objects — gravel, tire fragments — become projectiles capable of serious injury.

## Warning Signs Your Vehicle Is Failing

![Electronic Throttle Control (ETC) and Check Engine warning lights on a car dashboard](/blog/day38/dashboard-warning-lights.webp)

Don't wait for the engine to smoke. Exit the stream of traffic the moment you notice any of these:

- **Electronic Throttle Control (ETC) Light:** Your vehicle may enter "limp mode," dramatically and suddenly reducing speed — a dangerous condition in fast-moving traffic.
- **Abnormal Tire Noise:** A rhythmic thumping or slapping sound indicates an imminent blowout. Pull over before it happens.
- **Steam from the Hood:** Cooling system failure progresses to total engine seizure within minutes if ignored.
- **Brake Fade:** A soft or "spongy" pedal means your stopping power is severely compromised. This is an immediate emergency.
- **Battery or Alternator Warning:** You have a limited window before your headlights, ignition, and electronics fail completely — stranding you in the dark.

## 7 Immediate Safety Protocols

In the US, your survival depends on two things: visibility and distance from live traffic.

### 1. Activate Hazard Lights Immediately
The moment you suspect a problem — before you have confirmed it — activate your hazards. Do not wait. This is your first line of defense and buys critical reaction time for drivers behind you.

### 2. Exit to the Right Shoulder
Aim for the right shoulder. If you are in the left lane and cannot safely merge all the way across, move as far left as possible — but understand that the left-lane shoulder is significantly more dangerous than the right. Signal, check mirrors, and move progressively.

### 3. Stay Buckled Inside If You Cannot Reach Safety
If you cannot safely reach a grass verge, guardrail, or off-ramp, **stay in your vehicle with your seatbelt fastened.** The vehicle's steel frame provides far more protection against a rear-end collision than your body in the open. This is a critical rule that contradicts many people's instincts to "get away from the car."

### 4. Pop the Hood or Display a White Cloth
If it is safe to briefly exit via the passenger side, raise the hood or hang a white cloth from the driver's window. This is the universally recognized signal for a disabled vehicle and may prompt other drivers and law enforcement to slow down and assist.

### 5. Deploy Flares or Reflective Triangles
Place flares or FMCSA-compliant warning triangles at least 100 feet behind your vehicle — *only* if you can do so without entering active lanes of traffic. Per updated 2026 FMCSA regulations, LED flares are now preferred over liquid-burning flares for commercial operators; LED equivalents are significantly safer and more visible for personal vehicles as well.

### 6. Call 911 and Your Recovery Provider
Report your location using mile markers visible on the roadside, the nearest exit number, or GPS coordinates from your phone. Do not rely solely on verbal descriptions of cross-streets on interstates. Give the dispatcher your direction of travel (northbound, southbound, etc.) for a faster response.

### 7. Do Not Return to the Vehicle Until Professionals Arrive
Once you are safely behind a guardrail or on a verge, do not go back to the vehicle to retrieve items. If a vehicle slides onto the shoulder, the number one cause of secondary fatalities is people standing near or walking back to their car.

## Rules & Regulations: TRAA and FMCSA Compliance

The **Towing and Recovery Association of America (TRAA)** sets the national standard for roadside professionalism. Certified technicians must complete the National Driver Certification Program and follow **Traffic Incident Management (TIM)** protocols, which define how control zones are established, how responders approach a scene, and what PPE must be worn at all times.

Under **FMCSA regulations** (updated for 2026), commercial motor vehicles and tow trucks must adhere to strict electronic logging and medical certification standards. Federal "Move Over" advocacy has led to strengthened state laws — California's 2026 expansion, for example, now requires drivers to move over for *any* stopped vehicle displaying hazard lights, not just emergency responders. Additionally, **NHTSA** is actively phasing in mandatory Automatic Emergency Braking (AEB) requirements to reduce the frequency of high-speed rear-end collisions on major highways.

- [ ] Hazard lights on before the car is fully stopped?
- [ ] Pulled to the right shoulder, as far as possible?
- [ ] Stayed buckled if unable to reach safety?
- [ ] Hood up or white cloth displayed?
- [ ] Flares/triangles deployed at 100+ feet?
- [ ] Location reported to 911 using mile markers or GPS?
- [ ] Waiting behind guardrail — NOT standing next to vehicle?
