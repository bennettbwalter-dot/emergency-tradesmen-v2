## 1. Identification: How to Spot a Gas Leak
Natural gas in its raw state is completely odorless, colorless, and tasteless. To make it detectable to humans, utility companies add a chemical called **Mercaptan**.

### The Three Senses of Gas Safety
*   **Smell:** The most common sign is the "Rotten Egg" or sulfur smell. If the smell is faint, it may be a pilot light out. If it is overpowering, the leak is major.
*   **Sound:** Listen for a high-pitched hissing or whistling sound. This indicates gas escaping from a high-pressure line or a failed regulator.
*   **Sight:** Look for physical changes in your environment. Underground line leaks can cause dead patches of grass in an otherwise green yard, or a "cloud" of dust/mist blowing up from the soil. In some cases, you may see a white cloud around the gas meter itself.

## 2. The "No Spark/No Flame" Rule: Why Your Home is a Bomb
Natural gas is explosive when its concentration in the air is between 5% and 15%. This is a small window, and in an unventilated room, it can be reached in minutes. 

### Critical Prohibitions:
If you suspect a leak, do NOT perform any action that could create even a microscopic spark:
*   **Light Switches:** Do NOT turn lights ON or OFF. Internal arcing in a switch can ignite the gas.
*   **Telephones:** Do NOT use a landline or even a cell phone inside the home. The tiny electrical pulses in a phone can be a trigger.
*   **Garage Doors:** Do NOT use the electric garage door opener. The heavy-duty motor creates massive sparks during startup.
*   **Appliances:** Do NOT start or stop a dishwasher, washing machine, or dryer.
*   **Vehicles:** Do NOT start your car if it is parked in a garage attached to the house where the smell is present.

## 3. Evacuation & The "Golden Rule"
**Get out first. Investigate second.** 
1.  **Leave immediately:** Take your family and pets and walk at least 100 feet away from the structure.
2.  **Leave the door open:** This helps ventilate the house.
3.  **Call 911:** Once you are safely away from the house, call 911 or your local gas utility's emergency line (e.g., PG&E, ConEd, or Atmos Energy).

## 4. How to Shut Off Your Gas Meter (Technical Guide)
In the US, you should only attempt to shut off the gas if the meter is outside and it is safe to do so.

### The Tools and The Action
*   **Location:** Most US gas meters are located on the side or front of the house.
*   **The Valve:** Look for a pipe coming out of the ground into the meter. There is a valve (the "Stopcock") with a rectangular tab.
*   **The Rotation:** Use a 12-inch adjustable wrench. Turn the tab 90 degrees until it is **perpendicular (crosswise)** to the pipe. This indicates the gas is OFF.
*   **The Warning:** Once you shut off the gas, **Never** attempt to turn it back on yourself. In many states, this is illegal. A licensed plumber or utility technician must perform a pressure test before gas can be restored.

## 5. Carbon Monoxide (CO): The Silent Killer vs. Natural Gas
Many US homeowners confuse a gas leak with a Carbon Monoxide (CO) emergency. 

| Feature | Natural Gas Leak | Carbon Monoxide (CO) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Odor** | Rotten Eggs / Sulfur | Odorless |
| **Color** | Colorless | Colorless |
| **Symptom**| Immediate smell | Headaches, Nausea, Dizziness |
| **Source** | Pipes, Valves, Stove | Cracked Furnace Heat Exchanger |
| **Danger** | Explosion / Fire | Asphyxiation / Poisoning |

**US State Laws:** Most states (including CA, NY, IL, and TX) now legally require CO detectors on every level of a home and within 15 feet of every sleeping area.

## 6. Case Study: The "Pilot Light" Panic in Dallas
*   **The Scenario:** A homeowner returned from vacation to a faint gas smell in the kitchen. Panicked, they called the fire department.
*   **The Investigation:** The Fire Marshal found that the "Standing Pilot" on an old 1980s stove had blown out due to a draft from an open window.
*   **The Result:** The gas level was only 0.5% (Safe). The tech relighted the pilot and checked the ventilation.
*   **The Lesson:** Even a small leak can be scary, but following the "No Spark" rule prevents it from becoming a disaster.

## 8. 12-Month US Gas Safety Calendar
*   **Spring:** Check the gas meter for rust or damage. Ensure no shrubbery is blocking access for emergency crews.
*   **Summer:** If using a gas grill, check the hose for "Dry Rot" by spraying it with soapy water (bubbles = leak).
*   **Fall:** Get your furnace professionally serviced. This is the #1 way to prevent CO leaks.
*   **Winter:** Keep the external exhaust vents for your furnace and water heater clear of snow and ice.
*   **All Year:** Check the expiration date on your CO detectors. Most only last 7–10 years.

## 9. Specialized US Gas Glossary
1.  **BTU (British Thermal Unit):** The unit used to measure gas heat output.
2.  **CSST (Corrugated Stainless Steel Tubing):** Flexible yellow gas lines used in modern US homes.
3.  **Flammable Range:** The 5% to 15% concentration where gas can explode.
4.  **Hissing:** Gas escaping under high pressure.
5.  **Mercaptan:** The chemical that makes gas smell bad.
6.  **PSI (Pounds per Square Inch):** Gas pressure measurement.
7.  **Regulator:** The device on your meter that lowers the pressure for home use.
8.  **Riser:** The pipe coming out of the ground into your meter.
9.  **Shut-off Valve:** The manual lever to stop gas flow.
10. **Therm:** A unit of heat equal to 100,000 BTUs (used for billing).

## 10. Master FAQ: Top US Homeowner Questions
**Q: Can a gas leak happen in the yard?**
A: **Yes.** Underground pipes can corrode or be hit by "digging." In the US, always call **811** before you dig to have your lines marked for free.

**Q: Why does my house smell like gas only when the furnace turns on?**
A: This often indicates a "Delayed Ignition" or a cracked heat exchanger. Gas is being released but not lighting immediately. Turn the furnace off and call an HVAC pro.

**Q: Is propane the same as natural gas?**
A: **No.** Propane is heavier than air and will pool on the floor or in the basement. Natural gas is lighter than air and rises. Propane leaks are significantly more dangerous in basements.
